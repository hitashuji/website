Alaya by Mountain Native

Chamba, Himachal Pradesh · —

Brief

Above the terraced slopes of Chamba, Alaya sits low and heavy in the land, built the way this valley has always built — dry stone walls laid course by course, slate roofs pitched steep against the snow, timber gables carved out of the stonework like the house grew a second skin.

About the project

This is kath-kuni construction read through a contemporary eye: alternating layers of stone and deodar, load-bearing and load-sharing, a technique older than most architecture and still, here, the smartest answer to a Himalayan winter.

The cluster reads as a hamlet rather than a single building — three or four distinct volumes stepped across the terraces, linked by stone stairs and open ground, each roofline pitched at its own angle so the whole settlement looks less designed than accumulated over generations. At dusk, lit from within, it could pass for a village that simply happened to arrange itself well.

Weight and light, balanced. Where the stonework is solid and grounding, the timber does the opposite — glazed gables, shuttered windows, and open-air lounges that pull the pine forest and the valley haze straight into the room. A sunroom lined in floor-to-ceiling glass, ringed by low wooden benches, turns the view into the only furniture that matters.

Interiors built slow. Inside, mud-plastered walls and reclaimed pine hold a hush that new construction rarely manages. Four-poster beds draped in raw cotton, slate-topped tables, hand-lit lanterns — nothing here is bought to look old; it's built the way old things are built, and left to age honestly. A mezzanine loft, reached by an open timber stair, turns even a single room into a small journey.

Terrain as material. The house doesn't override the hillside's terraces — it uses them, letting each level of the building sit on its own natural shelf, stitched together by stone steps that could just as easily belong to the surrounding fields.

Alaya is Mountain Native's argument that vernacular building, done without irony, is still the most sophisticated response to this landscape — stone for the winters, timber for the light, and a plan that lets Chamba's terrain do half the design work.
